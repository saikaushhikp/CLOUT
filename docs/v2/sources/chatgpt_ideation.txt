Dumbocracy: Game design for the first two objectives
I would build this as a 5-minute election strategy game with a network of voters, opinion dynamics, limited campaign resources, and incomplete information.
Your vector-based idea is a good foundation. I would retain it, but make one important modification:
A voter's decision should depend on both their intrinsic policy preferences and the opinions of people around them.
This gives us three interesting dynamics:
Ideological alignment: How closely does the party's manifesto match the voter?

Social influence: What are the voter's friends, family, and community members thinking?

Media influence: How much has the voter been exposed to campaign messaging?

The player doesn't know every voter's preferences. They have to spend resources to understand and influence the electorate.
The design below follows the proposal's first two objectives: graph-based electorate and resource-consuming campaign/media units (pages 1–2). Dumbocracy.pdf

10. Important design choices to settle before coding
There are three things I would like to be particularly careful about.
A. Should voters' intrinsic preferences change?
My recommendation: No. Keep vi\mathbf v_ivi​ fixed throughout the game. Only their opinion xix_ixi​ changes.
This distinguishes what people fundamentally care about from which party they currently support.
B. Should the player be allowed to change the manifesto?
My recommendation: Keep the manifesto fixed during a game. Allow the player to choose campaign emphasis, but don't let them change their fundamental policy positions every round.
Otherwise, the game becomes an exercise in constantly changing ideology rather than campaign strategy.
C. Should social influence be symmetric?
My recommendation: Initially, yes. Use an undirected graph with symmetric edge weights.
Later, we can introduce influential individuals (opinion leaders) with higher outgoing influence.

My overall recommendation
The most important thing is to make the game about decision-making under partial information, rather than simply maximizing a mathematical score.
A good player should have to reason about questions like:
Should I spend 15 resources on a survey or 20 on workers?

Is a community genuinely opposed to me, or am I simply not reaching it?

Should I target a large community with weak support or a small community with strong potential?

Is media more effective than local campaigning in this particular election?

Can I trigger a social cascade by influencing a few strategically positioned voters?

These are the decisions that will make the MAS aspect meaningful.
One final suggestion: Keep the underlying simulation relatively sophisticated, but make the user-facing actions extremely simple: four campaign buttons, a map of the electorate, and a dashboard. The complexity should emerge from how the agents interact, not from the number of controls the player has to understand.
This also gives you a natural foundation for the proposal's later objectives: studying optimal strategies, partial observability, and whether victory can be guaranteed under different information conditions. Dumbocracy.pdf
